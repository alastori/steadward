## 1. Narrative Philosophy

### How story works in Steadward

Steadward does not have a plot. It has pressure, consequence, and memory.

The story of any given run is not authored by the writer. It is authored by the sequence of decisions the player made, the leaders they trusted, the crises they absorbed or ignored, and the resources they watched bleed out or stabilize. The writer's job is to make that sequence feel like a story — to ensure that when a player finishes a run, they can narrate what happened to someone else in a way that sounds like a tale rather than a spreadsheet.

This is emergent narrative: the game provides the vocabulary (leader personalities, event descriptions, outcome text, resource names), and the player constructs the grammar through play. Like Dwarf Fortress generates legends from systems colliding, or like XCOM turns procedural soldiers into people you mourn — Steadward turns weekly management cycles into a story about what you built and what it cost.

The writer seeds narrative potential. The systems deliver narrative meaning.

### The role of text in a dashboard game

Steadward's screen is dense. Every pixel serves information. There is no splash art, no dialogue box with a character portrait filling half the screen, no scrolling lore entry. Text lives alongside numbers, bars, icons, and cards. It competes for attention with mechanical information the player needs to make decisions.

This means every word must do two things simultaneously:

1. **Inform** — convey mechanical consequence clearly enough to support decision-making
2. **Evoke** — carry enough character, texture, or implication to make the player feel something

A description that says "Increases Materials by 15" informs but does not evoke. A description that says "The western ridge holds iron deposits. Mining them means sending crews into unstable terrain" informs AND evokes. The second version tells the player what will happen, but also makes them feel the weight of the decision.

Into the Breach proved that strategy games can carry emotional weight with almost no text at all — a pilot's one-line death quote hits harder than most RPG monologues because the systems have already made you care. Steadward follows this principle: systems create the emotional stakes, text gives them a voice.

### What the writing sounds like

Steadward's voice is the voice of a competent person under real pressure — not panicking, not grandstanding, not narrating their own heroism. It is the voice of someone reading a situation report, making a hard call, and moving on. It is dry without being cold. Direct without being blunt. Occasionally wry, never sarcastic.

Think: the calm voice on a space station intercom during a slow-developing emergency. The person who writes clear memos. The leader who says "we have a problem" instead of "oh god we're doomed."

**It sounds like:**
- A field report from someone who respects your time
- Notes in the margin of an engineering document
- The steady voice in mission control, not the astronaut screaming

**It does not sound like:**
- A fantasy narrator setting a scene
- A corporate press release
- A tutorial robot explaining things to a child
- A grimdark survivor monologuing about despair
- A cheerful game host congratulating the player

### What kind of frontier is this?

Steadward is set in a **post-expansion frontier** — not the first wave of colonization, but not an established civilization either. Humanity has spread to other worlds. Some settlements thrived. Some collapsed. Steadward is the name of a settlement that has survived its founding crisis but has not yet proven it can sustain itself. The player arrives (or is appointed) at the point where survival is no longer the question — sustainability is.

This is not a survival game. It is a *governance* game. The settlement has shelter, power, basic food production. What it lacks is coordination, institutional trust, and the ability to function without someone watching every process. The player's job is to build a system that works without them — and then, implicitly, to leave.

The frontier is science fiction, but not flashy. No aliens, no faster-than-light drama, no chosen-one mythology. The technology is functional and slightly worn. Think: prefab structures with condensation on the walls, repurposed cargo containers as offices, communication arrays that work most of the time. The aesthetic matches the art direction's muted blues and grays — competent engineering, not sleek futurism.

## 2. World-Building Foundation

### The setting

**Planet**: Sable. A terrestrial world with breathable atmosphere, harsh seasons, and enough raw material to sustain a settlement — if you know where to dig and when to stop. Sable is not hostile the way a vacuum or an ice world is hostile. It is indifferent. It produces dust storms, not monsters. Its dangers are logistical, not dramatic.

**How we got here**: Sable was one of dozens of colony targets identified during a period of rapid expansion. The expansion was driven by necessity — overcrowding, resource depletion, political fragmentation on the homeworlds. Colony ships carried prefabricated settlement kits and too many people with too few overlapping skills. Some colonies succeeded. Some went silent. Sable's settlement, Steadward, falls in the uncertain middle.

**Why we're here, specifically**: The player is not a founder. The founders did their work — they established the initial structures, drilled the first wells, survived the first winter. But the founders burned out, fought each other, or moved on. What remains is a settlement of roughly 200 people with functional infrastructure and no functional governance. The player is the coordinator brought in (or elected, or left standing) to make this place work as a system rather than as a collection of individual crises.

**What happened before**: The settlement's early history is not detailed for the player. It surfaces only through leader dialogue and event descriptions — references to "the first winter," "the Soral incident," "when the relay went down." These fragments create the sense of a shared past without requiring the player to read a codex entry.

### Technology level

**Practical science fiction.** The technology is roughly analogous to what you'd get if you took modern industrial capability and transplanted it to a planet with limited manufacturing infrastructure. Power comes from solar arrays and a small fusion plant that needs constant maintenance. Communications work via a relay network that covers the settlement and nearby survey zones but not the full planet. Construction uses prefab modules, local stone, and recycled ship components. Medical care is competent but stretched thin.

There are no robots doing the work. No AI running the systems. No nanomachines. The technology is **tool-level**, not **magic-level**. It breaks, it needs parts, it requires people who understand it.

This matches the art direction's principle: "closer to a Bloomberg terminal than a fantasy RPG." The technology is visible in the UI as data feeds, status indicators, and resource tracking — not as glowing crystals or holographic displays.

### The settlement

**Steadward** is a compact settlement built around a central operations hub. It is not a city. It is not a village. It is something like a research station that grew too large for its original purpose — a cluster of interconnected modules, workshops, greenhouses, and storage bays arranged along a loose grid.

The settlement is organized into four departments, each responsible for a domain:

- **Operations**: Day-to-day function. Power, water, food distribution, work schedules, equipment maintenance.
- **Infrastructure**: Building, repair, expansion. Physical plant, construction crews, material stockpiles.
- **Research**: Knowledge acquisition. Surveying, cataloguing local resources, adapting technology to local conditions, training.
- **External Affairs**: Everything outside the settlement walls. Scouting, trade with other settlements (if any survive nearby), managing the relationship between Steadward and the wider world.

### What's outside

Beyond the settlement perimeter: dust plains, rocky ridges, seasonal rivers, and weather systems that can shut down outdoor work for days. The landscape is not barren — there is life on Sable, mostly microbial and fungal, with some hardy plant analogs. But it is not lush. The color palette outside is ochre, gray, and the pale blue of a thin sky.

Other settlements may exist within communication range. Their status is uncertain. Trade is possible but unreliable. The broader colonial administration, whatever remains of it, is distant — measured in months of travel time, not hours. Steadward is functionally alone.

**The pressure is not a monster at the gate.** The pressure is entropy. Systems degrade. People get tired. Knowledge is lost when someone leaves or burns out. Materials run low. Trust erodes when things go wrong and nobody explains why. The threat is not dramatic — it is the slow unwinding of a system that needs constant attention to hold together.

This is the core tension of the game, and the core tension of the narrative: **Can you build something that holds together without you?**

## 3. Tone of Voice Guide

### The correct tone — examples

These sentences demonstrate what Steadward text should sound like. Each one could appear in the game as an event description, initiative outcome, or leader report.

1. "The water recycler in Module 7 is losing efficiency. Fen estimates two weeks before it drops below acceptable output."

2. "Survey team returned from the eastern ridge. Viable copper deposits confirmed, but the access route crosses unstable ground."

3. "Morale in Operations is steady. Not high — steady. People know what they're doing this week. That counts for something."

4. "Osei made the call to reroute power from the greenhouses to keep the relay online. The crops will recover. Probably."

5. "Three families requested transfers to the southern module. No complaints filed — they just want more space. Reasonable, if we had any."

6. "The storm passed. Structural damage is minor. The real cost was two days of outdoor work lost."

### What to avoid — examples

These sentences demonstrate tones, styles, and approaches that are wrong for Steadward.

1. "ALERT! Critical failure detected in water recycling subsystem! Immediate action required!" *(Too alarming, too robotic, too many exclamation marks.)*

2. "The brave settlers of Steadward face yet another challenge as the unforgiving world of Sable tests their resolve once more." *(Narrator voice. Nobody in the settlement talks like this.)*

3. "Lol the recycler broke again. Classic Module 7." *(Too casual, breaks immersion, undermines stakes.)*

4. "You have successfully completed the Water Infrastructure initiative. +15 Materials have been added to your inventory." *(Pure game-speak. No texture, no world.)*

5. "In the grim darkness of the frontier, there is only survival. The weak perish. The strong endure. Such is the way of Sable." *(Grimdark posturing. Steadward is about competence under pressure, not nihilism.)*

6. "Great job, Commander! Your leadership has inspired the whole settlement! Keep up the amazing work!" *(Patronizing. Steadward never flatters the player.)*

### Writing rules

| Rule | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Active voice. Present tense for current state, past tense for outcomes.** | Matches the dashboard's real-time feel. "The relay is down" not "The relay has been experiencing downtime." |
| **No exclamation marks in narrative text.** | Urgency comes from the systems (red borders, critical alerts), not from punctuation. The text stays calm so the player can think. |
| **Second person ("you") only in direct mechanical instructions.** | The game addresses the player as a peer, not as an audience. Event text uses third person or impersonal constructions. "The crew needs a decision" not "You must decide." |
| **Sentence fragments are acceptable.** | "Steady, not great." "Two weeks, maybe three." Fragments create rhythm and imply a speaker who is conserving words. |
| **Numbers under 10 are spelled out in narrative text, numerals in mechanical text.** | "Three families" in a description, "3 Attention" in a cost display. This separates story layer from system layer. |
| **No jargon without grounding.** | If the text says "the relay," the player should understand from context that it means the communication system. No glossary required. |
| **Leaders speak in character.** | Delegation reports and event responses reflect the leader's personality. Maren is terse. Osei is measured. Fen hedges. The voice is theirs, not the narrator's. |
| **Consequences, not judgment.** | Outcome text describes what happened, not whether it was good or bad. "Trust dropped. The Operations crew feels unheard." Not "You made a bad decision and morale suffered." |
| **One idea per sentence.** | Dense screens need scannable text. Complex sentences with multiple clauses force the player to re-read. |
| **Contractions are fine.** | "It's holding" is warmer than "It is holding." The settlement's voice is professional but human. |

### Tone shifts across modes

The game cycles through four modes, and the text subtly shifts its register to match.

**Observe** — Analytical. Descriptive. Status-reporting. The text in Observe mode reads like a briefing. It presents facts, trends, and warnings without urgency. This is the mode where the player reads and absorbs.
> "Infrastructure health is at 64 and declining. Two maintenance requests are pending. The southern wall repair has stalled — Fen's crew is waiting on materials."

**Plan** — Deliberative. Conditional. The text presents options and tradeoffs. It uses words like "could," "if," and "depending on." This is the mode where the player weighs.
> "Expanding the greenhouse requires three weeks and significant material investment. If overseen directly, yields are higher. Delegated to Maren, completion is reliable but output will be conservative."

**Execute** — Present-tense. Immediate. Shorter sentences. Things are happening. The text reports actions as they unfold and events as they interrupt.
> "Storm warning. Eastern sensor array is offline. Osei is rerouting the survey team. The greenhouse expansion continues on schedule."

**Review** — Reflective. Past-tense. Summative. The text looks back at the week and describes what changed. It has room for a slightly more considered tone.
> "The storm cost us two days of outdoor work but no structural damage. Osei's reroute kept the survey team safe. Trust held steady. The greenhouse expansion is 60% complete."

## 4. Leader Profiles

### Maren Stahl — Operations Lead

**Title**: Director of Operations
**Department**: Operations

**Personality snapshot**: Maren ran logistics for three colony expeditions before Steadward. She has seen what happens when systems fail and has no patience for optimism that isn't backed by a plan. She is the person who makes sure the lights stay on, the water flows, and the work schedule holds. She is not warm, but she is fair, and the Operations crew trusts her because she has never asked them to do something she wouldn't do.

**Voice**: Clipped. Practical. States facts, expects action. Does not soften bad news. Occasionally lets a dry observation slip through that passes for humor.

**Key tension**: Maren's reliability is also her limitation. She optimizes for stability, not growth. She will never take a risk that might break what already works. High judgment, high reliability, low risk tolerance — she is the leader you assign when you need something done right, and the leader who will resist anything experimental.

**Stats connection**: Judgment 8, Speed 5, Reliability 9, Adaptability 4, Communication 6, Risk Tolerance 3. She is slow to start but almost never fails. Her low adaptability means she struggles when plans change mid-execution. Her adequate communication means she'll report problems clearly but won't rally people through a crisis with inspiring words.

**Example lines**:

*Delegation success*:
> "Recycler maintenance is done. Replaced the filter assembly and recalibrated flow rates. Should hold for six weeks."

*Delegation partial success*:
> "We got the wall patched. Not pretty, but it's structural. I had to pull two people off the water crew to finish on time. They'll need to catch up next week."

*Delegation failure*:
> "I told you the timeline was too tight. We're halfway through and the material estimate was wrong. I need another week or I need to cut scope."

*Event response*:
> "The storm isn't the problem. The problem is we used our backup seals three weeks ago and never restocked. I'll handle it, but this shouldn't have happened."

### Kael Osei — External Affairs Lead

**Title**: Director of External Affairs
**Department**: External Affairs

**Personality snapshot**: Osei was a field surveyor before the settlement needed someone to manage its outward-facing operations. He is calm under pressure, good at reading situations, and genuinely interested in what lies beyond the perimeter. He sees the settlement's future as dependent on connection — to other settlements, to the wider landscape, to trade routes that don't exist yet. He is the most optimistic leader on the team, which sometimes means he overcommits.

**Voice**: Measured. Considers before speaking. Uses complete sentences. Frames problems as questions rather than complaints. The kind of person who says "what if we..." instead of "we can't..."

**Key tension**: Osei's vision sometimes outpaces his capacity. He commits to expeditions and trade negotiations that stretch his small team thin. High judgment, high communication, moderate speed — but his adaptability and risk tolerance mean he'll take on more than is safe if he believes in the outcome.

**Stats connection**: Judgment 7, Speed 6, Reliability 6, Adaptability 8, Communication 9, Risk Tolerance 7. His high communication means delegation reports are detailed and honest. His adaptability means he recovers well when things go sideways. His moderate reliability means that ambitious plans sometimes slip.

**Example lines**:

*Delegation success*:
> "Survey team reached the northern basin. Water source confirmed — seasonal, but predictable. I've marked three potential relay points along the route."

*Delegation partial success*:
> "We made contact with the Ardmore group. They're interested in trade, but cautious. I may have promised more material than we can spare right now. We should talk about that."

*Delegation failure*:
> "The eastern expedition had to turn back. Weather closed in faster than the forecast suggested. No one's hurt, but we lost the equipment cache we staged at ridge marker four."

*Event response*:
> "I know the timing is bad, but the signal from the Ardmore settlement is getting weaker. If we don't respond soon, they'll assume we've gone silent too."

### Dr. Fen Vasara — Research Lead

**Title**: Research Director
**Department**: Research

**Personality snapshot**: Fen is the settlement's most qualified scientist and its most frustrating leader. She is brilliant at analysis, meticulous in documentation, and constitutionally incapable of giving a simple answer. She sees complexity in everything, which makes her invaluable for research and maddening for anyone who needs a quick decision. She cares deeply about getting things right, and this care manifests as caution that others sometimes mistake for indecision.

**Voice**: Precise. Qualified. Uses words like "likely," "approximately," and "depending on variables." Adds caveats to good news and context to bad news. Speaks in the passive voice more than the other leaders, then catches herself and corrects it.

**Key tension**: Fen's thoroughness is her strength and her bottleneck. She will never give you bad data, but she will take twice as long as anyone else to give you any data at all. High judgment, high adaptability, very low speed — she is the leader you assign to high-stakes research, and the leader who will still be "running final checks" when the deadline has passed.

**Stats connection**: Judgment 9, Speed 3, Reliability 7, Adaptability 7, Communication 5, Risk Tolerance 4. Her judgment is the highest on the team — delegated initiatives she oversees almost never produce wrong outcomes, but they take longer. Her low speed means she needs more time for everything. Her moderate communication means her reports are thorough but dense.

**Example lines**:

*Delegation success*:
> "The soil analysis is complete. Short version: the eastern plots are viable for grain cultivation, but only during the warm season. I've attached the full dataset — there are some mineral anomalies worth discussing when you have time."

*Delegation partial success*:
> "We catalogued the fungal specimens from the ridge survey. Most are inert. One strain shows potential for filtration applications, but I'd want to run three more test cycles before I'd call it promising. Preliminary, not conclusive."

*Delegation failure*:
> "The equipment failure set us back. I should have flagged the calibration issue earlier — I noticed drift in the readings last week but thought it was within tolerance. It wasn't. I'll need to redo the full sequence."

*Event response*:
> "The contamination in the water supply — it's not dangerous yet. I want to be clear about that. But the trend line is concerning, and I'd rather we act now than wait for the data to get worse."

### Rook Tannis — Infrastructure Lead

**Title**: Director of Infrastructure
**Department**: Infrastructure

**Personality snapshot**: Rook built things on three different worlds before settling at Steadward, and he builds like someone who's seen his work torn down by weather, politics, and neglect. He is fast, confident, and willing to cut corners if the alternative is missing a deadline. He respects materials more than plans — give him enough steel and concrete, and he'll figure out the rest. He is the leader most likely to finish early and most likely to have left something out.

**Voice**: Blunt. Short sentences. States what he's doing, not what he's thinking. Rarely explains his reasoning — assumes you'll see the result. Uses concrete nouns and action verbs. The least verbal leader on the team.

**Key tension**: Rook's speed comes at the cost of thoroughness. He finishes projects fast but his work sometimes needs follow-up repairs. High speed, high risk tolerance, lower reliability — he is the leader you assign when you're behind schedule, and the leader whose work you check twice.

**Stats connection**: Speed 9, Risk Tolerance 8, Judgment 5, Reliability 5, Adaptability 6, Communication 4. His speed means delegated initiatives complete faster. His risk tolerance means he'll attempt projects others would refuse. His lower reliability means outcomes have more variance. His low communication means delegation reports are sparse — you get results, not explanations.

**Example lines**:

*Delegation success*:
> "Module 12 is up. Sealed, powered, ventilated. Moved the storage overflow in already."

*Delegation partial success*:
> "Wall section is done. Had to use the backup sealant — it'll hold, but we should replace it with proper compound before winter."

*Delegation failure*:
> "Foundation cracked. Bad substrate — should have drilled test cores first. I can fix it, but I need materials I already used."

*Event response*:
> "The roof panel on Module 3 — I can patch it tonight or replace it properly in three days. Your call."

### Lin Seo-yun — Generalist / Floater

**Title**: Senior Coordinator
**Department**: None (assignable to any department)

**Personality snapshot**: Lin is the newest addition to the leadership team and the only one without a fixed department. She was an administrative coordinator on the colony ship and transitioned into a troubleshooting role at Steadward — the person you send to a department that needs help. She is good at everything and exceptional at nothing, which makes her the most versatile leader and the one most likely to be overworked. She is perceptive, diplomatic, and quietly exhausted.

**Voice**: Warm but professional. Acknowledges difficulty without complaining. Asks clarifying questions. Uses "we" more than "I." The leader most likely to mention how the crew is feeling.

**Key tension**: Lin's versatility means she is always needed somewhere, which means she accumulates fatigue faster than any other leader. She is the safety valve — the leader you assign when no one else fits — but overusing her risks burning out your most flexible resource. Moderate stats across the board, high communication, high adaptability.

**Stats connection**: Judgment 6, Speed 6, Reliability 7, Adaptability 9, Communication 8, Risk Tolerance 5. No stat above 9, none below 5. She is the leader who never fails catastrophically but never exceeds expectations either. Her high adaptability means she handles unexpected complications well. Her high communication means delegation reports always include useful context about team dynamics.

**Example lines**:

*Delegation success*:
> "The crew rotation is sorted. Moved Patel to the morning shift where she's more effective, and paired the new arrivals with experienced hands. Output should stabilize by midweek."

*Delegation partial success*:
> "We finished the inventory audit, but there are discrepancies in the medical supply counts. Could be logging errors, could be something else. I'd rather flag it than ignore it."

*Delegation failure*:
> "I couldn't get the teams aligned in time. Operations and Infrastructure are both claiming priority on the same material batch, and neither will budge. I need you to make the call on this one."

*Event response*:
> "The crew in Module 5 is rattled. The power fluctuation wasn't dangerous, but it was the third one this month and people are starting to wonder if anyone's actually fixing things. Might be worth a visit."

## 5. Initiative Card Writing

### Template

Every initiative card contains the following text fields:

| Field | Max length | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Name** | 4-5 words | Identifies the initiative. Should be concrete and specific, not abstract. "Expand Greenhouse Capacity" not "Agricultural Enhancement." |
| **Department** | 1 word | Which department owns this initiative. |
| **Description** | 2-3 sentences (40-60 words) | What this initiative does and why it matters. Must convey both mechanical effect and narrative texture. First sentence states the action. Second sentence provides context or stakes. Third sentence (if needed) adds a complication or condition. |
| **Overseen outcome** | 1-2 sentences (15-30 words) | What happens when the player directly oversees this initiative. Better outcome than delegated. Written in past tense as a result. |
| **Delegated outcome** | 1-2 sentences (15-30 words) | What happens when a leader handles this independently. Outcome quality varies by leader stats. Written in past tense. Shows what was gained AND what was compromised. |
| **Cost display** | Numeric | Attention cost and duration. Displayed as icons and numbers, not prose. |
| **Requirements display** | Numeric | Resource thresholds. Displayed as resource pills, not prose. |

### Writing guidelines

- **Names are noun phrases, not verb phrases.** "Water Recycler Overhaul" not "Overhaul the Water Recycler." The card is a thing, not a command.
- **Descriptions start with the concrete action.** What is physically happening? Who is doing what? Then zoom out to stakes.
- **Overseen outcomes are slightly warmer.** The player was there. They saw the details. The text can include a small observational detail that rewards attention.
- **Delegated outcomes include a tradeoff.** The leader did it their way. Something was gained, something was compromised or left incomplete. This is not punishment — it is the natural cost of not being everywhere at once.
- **Never use the word "bonus."** This is a world, not a game manual. Instead of "bonus Materials," say "additional salvage" or "surplus stock."

### Example cards

---

- **Department**: Operations
- **Cost**: 3 Attention | 2 weeks
- **Requires**: Materials 20, Knowledge 15
- **Description**: The Module 7 recycler is running at 70% efficiency. A full teardown and rebuild would restore it, but the unit can't be offline for more than three days without rationing water across the settlement.
- **Overseen outcome**: Recycler restored to full capacity. The rebuild revealed a corroded intake valve — replacing it now prevents a failure that would have hit in four weeks. Resilience +10, Materials -8.
- **Delegated outcome**: Recycler is functional. The crew replaced the obvious components but skipped the deep inspection to stay on schedule. Resilience +6, Materials -5. Recycler may need attention again sooner than expected.

## 6. Event Writing

### Template

Every interruption event contains the following text fields:

| Field | Max length | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Name** | 3-5 words | Short, descriptive. Names the situation, not the emotion. "Supply Convoy Delayed" not "Crisis at the Gates." |
| **Urgency** | 1 word | Ignorable, Pressing, or Critical. Determines visual treatment (border color, icon). |
| **Description** | 2-4 sentences (40-80 words) | What is happening. Establishes the situation clearly enough for the player to evaluate options. Written in present tense. Includes enough sensory or human detail to create texture without becoming a short story. |
| **Choice text** (per option) | 1 sentence name + 1 sentence consequence preview (15-25 words each) | What the player can do. The name is an action phrase. The preview honestly states the likely mechanical outcome wrapped in narrative language. |
| **Outcome text** (per option) | 2-3 sentences (30-50 words) | What happened as a result of the choice. Past tense. Describes consequence without judging the player's decision. Includes a human detail or concrete image when possible. |

### Writing guidelines for events

**Choice text should present tradeoffs clearly.** The player must understand what they are trading before they choose. This means mechanical information (resource costs and gains) is embedded in the narrative description, not hidden behind it. "Divert materials to emergency repairs" tells the player what resource is at stake. "Send a team to investigate" tells them attention and time are being spent.

The formula for good choice text: **Action + Cost + Likely Consequence.**

> "Reroute power from the greenhouses to stabilize the grid. Crops stall for a week, but the settlement keeps heat." (Player knows: food production paused, but critical system saved.)

**Outcome text should feel like consequences, not rewards or punishments.** The game does not moralize. It describes what happened. If the player chose to ignore a pressing event, the outcome text does not say "you neglected your people." It says "the pipe burst two days later. Repair costs doubled." The facts carry the moral weight.

Frostpunk's endgame judgment screen demonstrated both the power and the risk of this approach — players felt the weight of their decisions, but resented being explicitly told they "crossed the line." Steadward lets the consequences speak for themselves. The player supplies their own judgment.

**Event 1: Signal From Ardmore (Ignorable)**

**Urgency**: Ignorable
**Description**: A faint signal on the long-range relay. It matches the frequency used by the Ardmore settlement, two hundred kilometers east. The signal is too weak to decode fully, but the pattern suggests a routine status broadcast — not a distress call. Responding would require realigning the relay dish, which takes a crew off other tasks for half a day.

**Option A: Respond to the signal**
- Cost: 1 Attention
- "Realign the relay and attempt contact. Half a day of communications crew time."
- Outcome: "Contact established. Ardmore is alive — smaller than us, but stable. They're interested in trading surplus medical supplies for construction materials. A relationship worth maintaining." Trust +3, Knowledge +4, Clarity +2.

**Option B: Log it and move on**
- Cost: 0 Attention
- "Note the signal in the communications log. We have higher priorities this week."
- Outcome: "The signal is logged. No response sent. If Ardmore is broadcasting regularly, we'll pick them up again. If not, we may have missed a narrow window." No immediate resource change. (Hidden: reduces probability of future Ardmore trade event.)

**Option C: Boost the relay to scan for other signals**
- Cost: 2 Attention
- "Spend extra time scanning the full frequency range. There might be other settlements out there."
- Outcome: "The scan picked up two additional signals — both faint, both intermittent. One may be automated. The other has human-pattern modulation. We're not as alone as we thought." Knowledge +8, Clarity +5, Momentum +3. Relay needs recalibration next week.

**Event 2: Structural Crack in Module 3 (Pressing)**

**Urgency**: Pressing
**Description**: A hairline crack appeared in the load-bearing wall of Module 3 during last night's temperature drop. The module houses twelve people and the secondary medical bay. The crack is not immediately dangerous, but structural assessments suggest it will worsen with the next freeze-thaw cycle. That cycle is five days away.

**Option A: Emergency repair — full resources**
- Cost: 3 Attention
- "Pull a construction crew and materials for a proper structural repair. Module 3 stays operational throughout."
- Outcome: "Rook's team reinforced the wall with a steel brace and resealed the foundation joint. The repair is permanent. Module 3 is secure. The construction crew is now behind on the southern expansion by two days." Resilience +8, Materials -12. Active Infrastructure initiative delayed 1 week.

**Option B: Temporary patch — minimal disruption**
- Cost: 1 Attention
- "Apply a surface seal and monitor it. Good enough for now, but this is not a permanent fix."
- Outcome: "The patch is holding. Rook says it will last through one more freeze cycle, maybe two. Module 3 residents were not informed of the severity — they noticed the repair crew but not the urgency. Whether that was the right call depends on what happens next." Resilience +3, Materials -4. Event may recur in 3-4 weeks with higher urgency.

**Option C: Evacuate and seal the module**
- Cost: 2 Attention
- "Move everyone out of Module 3 and seal it until a proper repair can be scheduled. Safe, but disruptive."
- Outcome: "Twelve people relocated to shared quarters in Module 5. The overcrowding is immediately felt. Two of the displaced families had just settled into Module 3 after the last reshuffle — the frustration is visible. Module 3 sits empty, awaiting repair." Trust -5, Resilience +6. Module 3 offline until repaired.

**Event 3: Contaminated Water Supply (Critical)**

**Urgency**: Critical
**Description**: Routine testing detected microbial contamination in the primary water tank. Fen's preliminary analysis suggests the source is a biofilm breach in the intake pipe. The contamination is not yet at dangerous levels, but concentration is rising. At current rates, the water becomes unsafe to drink within 48 hours. The settlement has no secondary water supply large enough to sustain the full population.

**Option A: Shut down the supply and purge the system**
- Cost: 4 Attention
- "Take the entire water system offline, flush the contamination, and sterilize the intake. Forty-eight hours without running water."
- Outcome: "The purge worked. Water is clean. But two days without running water tested the settlement's patience. People rationed what they had. Tempers ran short in the queues. The system is safe now — the memory of the shortage will last longer than the crisis." Trust -6, Resilience +12, Momentum -5. Water system fully restored.

**Option B: Isolate the contaminated section and run partial supply**
- Cost: 3 Attention
- "Bypass the contaminated intake pipe and run the secondary lines at reduced pressure. Everyone gets water, but not enough."
- Outcome: "The partial supply kept basic needs covered. Fen's team isolated the breach and began targeted treatment. It took four days instead of two, and the reduced water pressure caused a backup in the recycler. The contamination is contained, not eliminated." Trust -2, Resilience +6, Materials -8. Event may recur if not followed by Water Recycler Overhaul initiative.

**Option C: Emergency filtration using research prototypes**
- Cost: 3 Attention
- "Deploy Fen's experimental fungal filtration units on the contaminated line. Untested at this scale, but it could solve both problems at once."
- Outcome (success — if Fungal Filtration Prototype completed): "The prototype filters held. Water quality stabilized within six hours. Fen is cautiously triumphant — this is the first real-world validation of her filtration research. The settlement just became less dependent on imported filter stock." Resilience +10, Knowledge +8, Trust +5. Fungal filtration proven viable.
- Outcome (failure — if prototype not completed or leader judgment low): "The filters clogged within four hours. The fungal cultures couldn't handle the contamination load at this scale. Water quality dropped further before the team switched to manual boiling. We lost a day." Trust -8, Resilience -3, Materials -6, Knowledge +3 (learned from the failure).

### Tutorial / onboarding copy approach

Steadward does not have a tutorial in the traditional sense. There is no tutorial character, no pop-up assistant, no "click here to continue" sequence. Instead, onboarding uses three techniques:

**1. First-week scaffolding.** Week 1 is structured as a guided cycle. Each mode presents slightly fewer options than a normal week, and the text in each mode is written to teach by describing what the player is looking at:

> *Observe mode, Week 1*:
> "Four departments. Each has a health score. Operations is stable. Infrastructure is flagged — the southern wall needs attention. Your job this week: figure out what to prioritize."

> *Plan mode, Week 1*:
> "Two initiatives available. You have 8 Attention to spend this week. Each initiative has a cost. Assign a leader, or oversee it yourself."

**2. Contextual labels.** Every UI element that might be unfamiliar includes a brief label visible on first encounter. These labels are part of the UI, not pop-ups:

> "ATTENTION — Your weekly budget. Spent on overseeing work and responding to events. Does not carry over."

> "TRUST — The settlement's confidence in leadership. Drops when problems go unaddressed. Reaches zero, you lose."

**3. Leader commentary.** In Week 1, leaders provide slightly more explanatory lines than in later weeks, naturally teaching the player about game concepts through in-character dialogue:

> Maren, Week 1: "If you oversee the wall repair yourself, it'll go better — but that's Attention you can't spend elsewhere. Or you could assign Rook. He's fast, but he cuts corners."

After Week 1, the scaffolding text is replaced by standard-length descriptions. The game trusts the player to learn by doing.

## 8. Writing Production Guide

### Total word count estimate

| Content category | Items | Words per item | Total estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initiative cards (descriptions + outcomes) | 15 | 120 | 1,800 |
| Event text (description + choices + outcomes) | 10 | 250 | 2,500 |
| Leader dialogue lines (all contexts) | 5 leaders x 15 lines | 25 | 1,875 |
| UI copy (screens, transitions, labels) | ~30 strings | 30 | 900 |
| Tutorial / onboarding text | ~15 strings | 40 | 600 |
| Contextual labels and tooltips | ~40 strings | 12 | 480 |
| Victory / loss / demo wall | 6 text blocks | 80 | 480 |
| **Total estimate** | | | **~8,600 words** |

This is deliberately compact. For reference, a single Frostpunk event chain contains more text than Steadward's entire event pool. The constraint is intentional — every word competes for space on an information-dense screen.

### How text is structured in code

All player-facing text lives in structured TypeScript objects within the `src/content/` directory. Text is never hardcoded in UI components.

**String format**: TypeScript objects with typed string fields, not raw JSON or Markdown. This allows type checking to catch missing text fields at compile time.

```typescript
// src/content/base-pack/initiatives.ts
export const INITIATIVE_WATER_RECYCLER: InitiativeContent = {
  id: "water-recycler-overhaul",
  name: "Water Recycler Overhaul",
  department: "operations",
  description: "The Module 7 recycler is running at 70% efficiency. A full teardown and rebuild would restore it, but the unit can't be offline for more than three days without rationing water across the settlement.",
  overseen_outcome: "Recycler restored to full capacity. The rebuild revealed a corroded intake valve — replacing it now prevents a failure that would have hit in four weeks.",
  delegated_outcome: "Recycler is functional. The crew replaced the obvious components but skipped the deep inspection to stay on schedule.",
  // ... mechanical fields (cost, duration, resources) are separate
};
```

```typescript
// src/content/base-pack/leaders.ts
export const LEADER_MAREN: LeaderContent = {
  id: "maren-stahl",
  name: "Maren Stahl",
  title: "Director of Operations",
  lines: {
    delegation_success: "Recycler maintenance is done. Replaced the filter assembly and recalibrated flow rates. Should hold for six weeks.",
    delegation_partial: "We got the wall patched. Not pretty, but it's structural.",
    delegation_failure: "I told you the timeline was too tight.",
    // ... keyed by context
  },
};
```

**String key convention**: All string keys use snake_case, prefixed by content type: `init_`, `event_`, `leader_`, `ui_`. This makes text extractable for localization via automated tooling.

### Localization considerations

**Design for extraction from day one.** Even though v1 ships in English only, the text architecture should make future localization straightforward:

1. **No string concatenation in UI code.** Never build sentences by combining fragments ("The " + leaderName + " completed " + taskName). Use template strings with named placeholders: `"${leader} completed ${task}"`. This allows translators to reorder elements for languages with different word order.

2. **Plan for 35% text expansion.** German and French text is typically 30-35% longer than equivalent English. UI components must accommodate longer strings without breaking layout. Test with pseudo-localization (replace characters with accented variants and pad strings by 35%) during development.

3. **Context comments for every string.** Translators need to know where text appears, what it refers to, and what constraints apply (character limits, tone). Include a `_context` field alongside each string:

```typescript
{
  text: "Read the ground.",
  _context: "Mode transition text shown when entering Observe mode. 3-4 words max. Tone: calm, analytical."
}
```

4. **Gendered language.** Leader names and pronouns are specified in leader data objects. Languages with grammatical gender will need leader gender markers to generate correct agreement. Store leader pronouns (`he/him`, `she/her`, `they/them`) in the content data.

5. **Pluralization.** Use ICU MessageFormat or equivalent for any string that includes counts: `"{count, plural, one {# week} other {# weeks}}"`. Do not assume English plural rules apply.

6. **Cultural sensitivity.** The setting is deliberately non-Earth, non-ethnic, non-religious. Leader names are designed to suggest diversity without mapping to specific real-world ethnicities. Avoid idioms that are English-specific ("cut corners," "burning the candle at both ends") in favor of concrete language that translates cleanly.

### Punctuation rules

- **Em dashes** (---) for parenthetical asides in narrative text. Use sparingly — more than one per paragraph is too many.
- **No semicolons** in player-facing text. They slow reading. Use a period and start a new sentence.
- **No ellipsis** (...) except in leader dialogue where it conveys hesitation (Fen only). Ellipsis in UI text looks unfinished, not dramatic.
- **No exclamation marks** in narrative text, event text, or outcome text. Acceptable only in critical alert labels ("CRITICAL") and never in body copy.
- **Oxford comma**: Yes. "Materials, Trust, and Clarity" not "Materials, Trust and Clarity."
- **Periods at the end of all full sentences**, including tooltip text and descriptions. No trailing periods on labels or headers.
